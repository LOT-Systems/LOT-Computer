## THE STOIC CODEX — THEME OVERVIEW

Stoic philosophy is the original self-care operating system. Marcus Aurelius wrote the
*Meditations* as a private journal — daily notes on how to live better, not abuse power,
stay present, and face death without flinching. Seneca wrote 124 letters to a friend about
reclaiming wasted time. Epictetus, born a slave, taught that the only thing that cannot be
taken from you is your will.

As a self-care vocabulary, Stoicism names the exact practices that journaling supports:
the morning review, the evening meditation, the dichotomy of control, equanimity under
pressure, amor fati — love of what is. These are not metaphors for wellness. They are
the original wellness philosophy, 2,300 years old and still operational.

## FLAVOR TEXT — THE STOIC CODEX

> *"You have power over your mind — not outside events. Realize this, and you will find
> strength." — Marcus Aurelius, Meditations. The practice is not about controlling the
> world. It is about controlling your response to it. That is enough.*

> *"It is not that we have little time, but that we waste so much of it." — Seneca,
> On the Shortness of Life. Time reclamation is the Stoic project. Every check-in is an
> act of time reclamation.*

> *"Seek not the good in external things; seek it in yourself." — Epictetus, Discourses.
> The dichotomy of control is the first principle of self-care. What is in your power?
> Your judgements. Your intentions. Your practice. Begin there.*

> *"He who fears death will never do anything worthy of a living man." — Seneca. Memento
> mori is not morbid — it is clarifying. It filters the noise. When you remember you will
> die, the trivial stops mattering.*

> *"Make the best use of what is in your power, and take the rest as it happens." —
> Epictetus. This is the entire philosophy in one sentence. The dichotomy of control,
> amor fati, and equanimity — all three in eighteen words.*
