## THE STOIC TERMINAL — THEME OVERVIEW

The Stoics were the original self-care engineers. Marcus Aurelius, emperor of Rome,
wrote the *Meditations* only for himself — private notes, never published, a daily
practice of self-examination in the shadow of empire. Epictetus was a slave who became
the most powerful teacher of his age. Seneca wrote letters about dying well while
living in the most dangerous court in Rome.

Their vocabulary was precise and functional: not mystical but operational. The dichotomy
of control. Amor fati. Memento mori. Logos. Praxis. These are not decorations — they
are tools. The Stoic Terminal is the LOT interface for that toolkit.

You write yourself into resilience. The terminal acknowledges it.

## FLAVOR TEXT — THE STOIC TERMINAL

> *"You have power over your mind — not outside events. Realize this, and you will find
> strength." — Marcus Aurelius. The terminal is not a productivity tracker. It is a
> mind tracker. The practice is in the naming.*

> *"Waste no more time arguing about what a good man should be. Be one." — Marcus
> Aurelius, Meditations X.16. The terminal does not argue. It acknowledges. The entry
> is the action.*

> *"He is a wise man who does not grieve for the things which he has not, but rejoices
> for those which he has." — Epictetus. The check-in is the rejoicing. The word is the
> inventory.*

> *"Amor fati — love of one's fate. That one wants nothing to be different, not forward,
> not backward, not in all eternity." — Nietzsche on the Stoic concept. When you name
> the hard thing, you have already turned toward it.*

> *"Per aspera ad astra — through hardships to the stars. The Stoics would have added:
> and the hardship is the star." — Seneca, Letters to Lucilius, adapted.*

> *"Begin at once to live, and count each separate day as a separate life." — Seneca.
> Every check-in is a life. Every journal entry is a life. The streak is not a streak.
> It is lives.*
